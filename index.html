Project 01

Turbofan Engine Reliability Study

System-level reliability of a turbofan built up from NASA C-MAPSS degradation data and a load-strength model of the turbine blade.

System MTTF
246 cycles
B10 life
161 cycles
Availability
80.4%
Blade reliability
0.834
Scope

Maintenance intervals set from average life ignore scatter in both loading and material strength, so operators either replace parts early or carry failure risk they cannot see.

Outcome

System MTTF of 246 simulation cycles and B10 life of 161 cycles at 80.4% availability. A preventive overhaul at B10 raised end-of-interval reliability from 0.8% to 13.2%, a gain of 12.4 percentage points.

Role

Team lead, 3-person team. Led the code and modeling work, and served as the primary point of contact with faculty and TAs.

What I did

  • Characterized component life from C-MAPSS time-to-failure data, fitting lognormal models to the fan and HPC (MTTF 348 and 283 cycles) and a Weibull rank regression to the LPT.
  • Quantified turbine blade reliability at 0.834 with a load-strength Monte Carlo model on Inconel 718, treating load at 400 MPa and strength at 480 MPa as distributions, a standardized safety margin of 0.971.
  • Produced R(t), hazard rate, MTTF, and B10 life for the engine by propagating component reliability through a fan, HPC, and 2-of-4 LPT system model.
  • Ranked improvement options on the same curve: a thermal barrier coating lifted blade reliability to 0.943 and system MTTF to 261 cycles, while overhaul at B10 traded availability for reliability.

  • Weibull analysis
  • Monte Carlo
  • NASA C-MAPSS

Project 02

UAV Corrosion Detection System

An S550 hexacopter that flies structural inspections and flags surface and filiform corrosion using onboard infrared imaging.

Stable flight time
9 minutes 30 seconds
Team size
5 engineers
Subsystems integrated
3 (flight, imaging, structure)
Scope

Corrosion surveys on elevated assets need scaffolding, rope access, or shutdowns, and filiform corrosion spreading beneath a coating stays invisible to a visual check until the paint lifts.

Outcome

Flight-ready platform carrying the full imaging payload, with a measured stable flight time of 9 minutes 30 seconds after the subframe redesign cut airframe mass and moved the payload closer to the thrust centroid.

Role

Team lead, five-person team. Senior capstone design project. Owned mechanical design and analysis.

What I did

  • Cut airframe mass and stiffened the payload mount by redesigning the S550 subframe in SolidWorks, verified against flight loads with static structural analysis.
  • Reached 9 minutes 30 seconds of stable flight by relocating battery and payload mass to bring the center of gravity into the thrust plane, checked with a mass-properties study and bench balance tests.
  • Validated the load path under landing and hard-maneuver cases with FEA, confirming safety factors on the arm-to-plate interfaces before flight testing.
  • Detected both surface and filiform corrosion by pairing infrared imaging with the OAK-D Pro stereo camera on a Pixhawk 4C controller, with vibration isolation sized to keep thermal frames usable.
  • Kept the team on schedule through design reviews and a documented build standard.

  • Static structural FEA
  • Pixhawk 4C
  • OAK-D Pro
  • Infrared imaging

Project 03

Thermo-Mechanical Brake Disc Analysis

A coupled thermal-structural FEA in Abaqus measuring what drilled cooling holes buy in heat rejection and cost in stress.

Peak temperature
23% lower
Peak stress
28.1% higher
Peak stress, 3 holes
1.588 × 108 Pa
Scope

Drilled discs shed heat faster but remove material and concentrate stress. The design question is whether the cooling benefit is worth the local stress penalty.

Outcome

Going from one to three drilled holes cut peak disc temperature by 23% but raised peak Von Mises stress at the hole edge from 1.24 × 108 Pa to 1.588 × 108 Pa, up 28.1%, moving the governing failure location to that edge as hole count rises.

Role

Team lead, 5-person team. Led design and simulation, and served as the primary point of contact with faculty and TAs.

What I did

  • Established a 23% reduction in peak disc temperature from one to three drilled holes by building a coupled thermal-structural model in Abaqus with frictional heat flux over the pad swept area.
  • Quantified the cost of that cooling at a 28.1% rise in peak Von Mises stress, from 1.24 × 108 Pa to 1.588 × 108 Pa at the hole edge.
  • Kept the thermal result physical rather than adiabatic by defining temperature-dependent material properties and convection boundary conditions.
  • Identified the hole edge as the governing failure location by post-processing stress fields against thermal gradients and confirming peak values were mesh independent under refinement.
  • Abaqus CAE
  • Coupled thermal-structural FEA
  • SolidWorks
  • Python post-processing

Project 04

3D-Printed Hydraulic Crane

A working crane with hydraulic actuation, printed in polymer and sized by hand calculation, proven first on a scaled-down prototype and then in simulation.

Material usage
18% lower
Factor of safety
2.0 maintained
Scope

Printed polymer members are compliant and buckling-prone, so a crane boom that carries a real hydraulic load has to be sized for deflection and stability, not just yield.

Outcome

Cut material usage by 18% while holding a factor of safety of 2.0 across the structure, and the crane lifted its design load with no field failures across demonstration cycles.

Role

Structural analysis and validation lead.

What I did

  • Cut material usage by 18% at a factor of safety of 2.0 by sizing boom and mast sections from beam deflection and Euler buckling limits before any part was printed.
  • Confirmed that safety factor in simulation with static stress and displacement studies in SolidWorks Simulation, cross-checking the critical load case in ANSYS.
  • Held bearing stress at the pin joints and cylinder mounts inside the printed material allowable at the same factor of safety, setting joint sizing rather than accepting the first geometry that fit.
  • De-risked the full-size build by printing a scaled-down prototype first to verify linkage motion and joint fit, then load tested the final crane.

  • ANSYS Mechanical
  • FDM printing
  • Hand calculation

Project 05

CFD Front Wing Study

A Formula-style front wing simulated in two configurations to put a number on the downforce cost of running low drag.

Downforce gain
43.9%
Pressure differential gain
531 Pa
Scope

Front wing setup is a trade: more downforce buys cornering grip and costs straight-line speed. The trade needs a number before it can be tuned.

Outcome

The high-downforce configuration produced a 43.9% downforce increase and a 531 Pa gain in pressure differential over the low-drag setup, with the drag penalty quantified for each case.

Role

Individual graduate project.

What I did

  • Quantified a 43.9% downforce increase and a 531 Pa pressure differential gain between low-drag and high-downforce configurations by running matched steady-state CFD cases at identical inlet conditions.
  • Established confidence in both numbers through mesh independence checks and residual and force-coefficient convergence monitors on the critical case.

  • CFD meshing
  • SolidWorks
  • MATLAB
